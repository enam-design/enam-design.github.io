DANCE COACH / Independent project · Open Vector curriculum / 2026

Designing and building an AI practice coach for Latin dance teams, without writing the code myself.

Dance Coach is a practice app for Latin dance teams. An instructor uploads a reference video of the week's choreography. Students record themselves, get instant AI feedback on timing and movement, and practice as many times as they want. They then submit a final take, and the instructor reviews it, adds notes, and releases feedback.

I designed and built it alone over about six weeks in summer 2026. I have no coding background, so I directed Claude Code through the Open Vector curriculum. The first iteration runs end to end in production: an Android app for students, a web dashboard for instructors, and a backend that analyzes pose data with MediaPipe.

I built Dance Coach as the project for working through the Open Vector curriculum, so the case study follows the curriculum's order. The build ran ahead of the research pipeline: I had a working app before I'd done Jobs to Be Done, ideation, or validation. When real student videos showed the AI feedback wasn't accurate enough to trust, I paused feature work and went back to the pipeline. This case study covers the curriculum up to that point and what I'm doing next.

01Objective

I built Dance Coach mainly to learn how to build with AI agents. I followed the Open Vector curriculum, which covers the whole process from idea to deployment. I also applied Anthropic's AI Fluency course to how I prompted and checked the agent's work.

I approached Dance Coach as an app I would actually want to release. Its main purpose, though, was hands-on practice with the Open Vector curriculum. So I put learning the concepts, practicing with tools like Claude Code, and having solid reasons for each design choice ahead of shipping a polished app. The app is still in progress, so this case study covers my process through the first iteration and my intended next steps.

What Open Vector taught before any building started. Open Vector sets itself against "vibe coding": prompting, seeing what comes out, and nudging until something works. It has you plan the system first, check the output, and understand why it works. The orientation and foundation units covered the terminal, file systems, Git, deployment, DNS, systems thinking, architecture, data modeling, and information architecture. They ended with a VECTOR.md file, a single document describing the product that the agent treats as the source of truth.

What AI Fluency added. The course's four skills shaped how I worked with Claude Code:

  • Delegation: deciding which work to give the agent and which to keep, such as domain judgment about dance technique
  • Description: writing prompts with enough context and constraints to build the right thing
  • Discernment: checking output instead of trusting it
  • Diligence: owning what ships, including security and accuracy

Why dance. I chose the dance space for two reasons:

  1. I have ready access to instructors and students in the Boston Latin dance community, where I've danced for over 8 years.
  2. Dance is a form of exercise and community, with physical and mental health benefits. This choice builds on my general interest in health and wellness.

My research was closer to guerrilla testing than to formal studies, and I designed the synthesis around that. I referred to "Just Enough Research" to optimize my research findings, given my constraints.

Everyone in the local dance community has a busy schedule, and my resources were limited, so I couldn't run formal, compensated sessions. My own positions, kept separate

Keeping my own view separate. My experience as a dance student drove the first hypothesis and the first designs to test. That is useful, but it is a bias risk. In the affinity map, I recorded my own positions as a separate, visibly marked source. I left them out of each group's source count and out of the insights.

What this research can and can't support. It is a convenience sample from one scene, and there was no common script. I steered the conversation based on the interviewee's background (are they a studio owner? a student wanting to skilll-up? a private instructor) to capture their point of view. So a source saying nothing about a topic usually means the topic never came up, not that they disagreed. The findings support deciding what to build and test next. They don't support sizing a market.

Method. I broke each interview summary into single observations and grouped them by similarity, regardless of who said them. Then I named each group and wrote insights with a confidence level based on how many independent sources agreed.

03Hypothesis and competitive landscape

Starting hypothesis: AI movement analysis could help dance students work on their technique between classes, as a supplement to instructors rather than a substitute.

Three observations shaped it:

  • Teams already share practice videos, but on a general-purpose tool. Teams post choreography, homework videos, and feedback on Band, alongside announcements and group chat. Nothing there analyzes the videos.
  • AI already assists expert judgment in other fields. AI grading tools in higher education give a first pass that a teacher reviews. I wanted the same model here: the AI does the repetitive work, and the instructor keeps ownership of the relationship.
  • Pose analysis is common in fitness and rare in dance. Fitness apps use it to check exercise form. Dance is harder to judge because correct form is less constrained. That gap looked like an opening.

Competitive analysis. Many virtual learning platforms teach dance, but I found only two that use AI movement tracking and analysis: DanceBetter AI and DanceBuddy. Neither is built around a team and its instructor, which is the gap Dance Coach targets.

Biggest takeaways from the competitive analysis.

  1. No competitor combines the three things Dance Coach does: the studio's own reference video, AI feedback, and instructor review. Every AI product is sold to individual learners and compares against generic dancers or a video the learner picks. The closest threat is DanceBetter's quote-only studio tier, which sends AI reports under the coach's name. It has no instructor review step and no team assignments.
  2. AI dance feedback has not shown that it works or that people will pay for it. DanceBetter has no reviews, no sample output, and in my July test it returned no result after a full day. DanceBuddy has about 10,000 users, zero paying customers, and no update since November 2025. Dance Coach's own accuracy isn't reliable yet either. So AI alone can't be the pitch. The instructor review step is what makes the product credible while the model improves.
  3. The real competitor is free solo practice, with Band alongside it. Students already rewatch class video, slow the music, and use a mirror, and it costs nothing. Band has 4.8 stars from 784k reviews and is already installed, even though students mute it. Dance Coach has to offer something solo practice can't, which is feedback. I still need to decide whether it sits next to Band or replaces it.
  4. Partner work is the largest functional gap. Most team material is partnered, according to Nick and Lumyr. Dance Coach only detects one person. DanceBetter claims to assess frame and connection from one video, but it shows no evidence.
  5. The value is speed and cost. Homework feedback through Band takes about a week. Private lessons give immediate feedback but cost $50–224/hr. Dance Coach sits between the two, and that is the case to make for pricing, which isn't set yet.
  6. Practice-player features are expected, and Dance Coach is missing most of them. VDanceClub and DanceBuddy both offer mirror, loop, and 8-count segments. Dance Coach only has speed control. These are cheap to add compared to the AI work.
  7. Nobody assesses named fundamentals or offers level placement. The course platforms teach fundamentals but never assess them. Scoring named steps, as SyC suggested, is an opening no one has taken.

Why choreography first. Three reasons: recurring weekly use, distribution through the instructor, and an easier cold start. I pushed back on the first reason while deciding. Leveling could also bring students back through progress on fundamentals, and that may be stickier because students choose it themselves. Both directions could keep students returning, just in different ways. Choreography was the better entry point, with leveling folded in later.

What the build later taught me. The technical work partly undermined that choice. Comparing choreography means aligning two videos filmed on different cameras, in different rooms, at different angles, and that is where most of the accuracy problems came from. Fundamentals are judged against fixed criteria: one video, with no alignment. SyC's instructors made the same point on their own: single named steps have clean framing and one clear target. I don't regret the choice, but the direction that looked harder to grow may have been easier to make accurate.

08Key design decisions

Four decisions defined the first iteration. Each one traded something away.

1. Unlimited AI practice before instructor review. Students can record as many practice takes as they want and see an AI score each time. They submit a final take only when they're satisfied.

  • Why: students get immediate feedback during the week, and instructors review one take per student instead of every attempt.
  • Tradeoff: students see AI feedback that no instructor has checked. This is the tension that later paused the project (section 11).

2. Two videos per piece: one to counts, one to music. This matches how the instructors I talked to already assign homework.

  • Why: the counts version isolates timing, and the music version shows musicality and styling.
  • Tradeoff: students record twice, and the instructor uploads two references.

3. Students on mobile, instructors on a web dashboard. Students record on their phones. Instructors review on a laptop.

  • Why: Lumyr reviews only on a laptop with two monitors, and a phone can't show detail or let him watch and type at once.
  • Tradeoff: two front ends to build and maintain. Students who prefer a larger screen to review (Nick, and me) still get phone only.

4. Roles verified on the server. The backend decides whether someone is a student or an instructor from a signed identity token (Clerk), never from what the app says. It checks the role again on every request.

  • Why: if the app could claim a role, any student could open the instructor's review queue and read other students' unreleased feedback. Before auth existed, that data was open to anyone with the URL.
  • Tradeoff: instructors are promoted by hand for now. That is deliberate gatekeeping at this scale, and invite codes could replace it later.
09Building with an AI agent

Claude Code wrote the code. My job was to set the plan, give context, and catch the agent when it was wrong. The problems I caught mattered more than the amount of code produced.

How I directed the build.

  • A sequence of prompts, run in order. I broke the build into numbered prompts, each one a feature with its dependencies stated. Examples: storage, then pose analysis, then the dashboard, then auth. Thirteen prompts produced the working core loop. I added a rule: any decision that changes a prompt I haven't run yet must be flagged, along with what needs updating.
  • VECTOR.md as the source of truth. One document described the users, the loop, the data model, and the current state. The agent read it at the start of every session, and I updated it after each milestone.
  • A check that the docs match the code. Partway through, I found VECTOR.md said the auth system was installed and that the user and team tables existed. Neither was true. I had Claude Code write a script that checks the doc's countable claims against the repo (migration count, installed dependencies, API routes), so that kind of drift fails a check instead of hiding.
  • An architecture review. I asked for a review of the whole system and tracked the results in ARCHITECTURE-DEBT.md, ranked by cost: four items closed, eleven open.

10Testing whether the AI feedback is right

The app worked end to end, but when I uploaded two real student videos, the feedback wasn't accurate enough to show anyone. I stopped adding features and focused on accuracy.

Found real differences: right shoulder, head stability (about 51% more head movement for the beginner), and spine tilt. It flagged core engagement without being asked.

What is still unreliable.

  • Style is scored as error. Accent ratio and shoulder ratio were the least trustworthy outputs. They measure stylistic choices, not mistakes.
  • The score measures how you move, not which step you do. I haven't yet run a deliberately wrong take to see whether the score is too generous toward wrong choreography.
  • Recording conditions distort results. Body type, distance from the camera, lighting, and framing all change how large a movement looks.
  • Failures look like bad dancing. One practice take scored 0 against another at 44, most likely because no pose was detected. A student who sees a 0 with no explanation would reasonably think the app is broken.
  • Count numbering assumes count 1 has the most movement. Choreography that accents other counts gets its counts misnumbered, so feedback can name the wrong count.

The key lesson. The beginner-vs-advanced run found the right answer on a case where the answer was already obvious. That is exactly the case where nobody needs the tool. Getting the obvious case right doesn't show the AI can be trusted on subtle cases.

11Pausing the build to finish the pipeline

On Aug 30, I paused feature work for two reasons. First, the project's purpose was to work through the Open Vector curriculum, and building had pulled me ahead of the pipeline. Second, I hadn't shown that the AI feedback is something people actually want. The technical problems can be solved, but whether the feedback is useful was the real unknown. The remaining pipeline steps, Jobs to Be Done through validation, are how I'll answer that.

The core design tension. The instructor's review is what makes the feedback safe, and the practice loop removes it.

  • When the instructor reviews every AI note before releasing it, wrong and style-only notes get filtered out. That loop works.
  • But half the value was that students could practice on their own without using the instructor's time. In practice mode, nothing filters the output. A student sees incorrect feedback as if it were correct, which is actively harmful.
  • The risk is becoming known as the app that gives wrong corrections.

I haven't resolved this yet. These are the options I'm weighing:

  1. Show students less than instructors see. Sort each measure into shown to students, shown to instructors only, or hidden. This is a product decision that doesn't depend on the model improving.
  2. Say plainly what the AI doesn't judge, such as style and partner connection, and let students flag or dismiss a note.
  3. Fix the input first: a framing guide while recording and a quality check before submitting ("good to submit" or "fix your lighting").
  4. Bring in a subject-matter expert, an instructor who can watch a video and name exactly what to correct. They would tell me which measures matter and where the AI is wrong.

What changed in how I work. Once building started, almost all my time went to coding and debugging. After Aug 30, I committed at least half my time on the project to user and business research. I also changed my interview question from "would you want this?" to "what do you need, and what would you pay?"

12Outcome, next steps, and what I learned

What works today. The full loop runs in production with real accounts on both sides. I verified it on a device in August. A student signs in, joins the team, sees the assignment, records and scores practice takes, and submits a final take. The instructor then reviews it and releases feedback, and the student reads it.

Limits.

  • Android only. There's no iPhone build, even though iPhone users are the larger group.
  • MediaPipe tracks one person, but teams move to group and partner practice as the season goes on.
  • The feedback isn't useful yet for intermediate or advanced dancers. Beginner teams are the likely first users.
  • Some measures score style as error, and a failed pose detection looks like a score of 0.

Next steps.

  1. Decide what practice mode shows students (section 11) before any more model tuning.
  2. Run a controlled accuracy test: the same dancer in the same clothes and lighting, including a deliberately wrong take.
  3. Test recording guidance and a quality check before submitting.
  4. Interview more students, including women and people who left their teams. So far most sources are men, and only one is a student.
  5. Later: test a step library built from the current choreography. It could satisfy both the fundamentals and the choreography positions from the research.

What I learned.

  • An AI agent builds fast, so deciding when to stop matters more. Once building started, it took all my time. The most important decision in the project was to stop and validate.
  • The agent is only as right as the domain knowledge I give it. It got bachata hip action and weight transfer wrong, and only my experience as a dancer caught that.
  • Docs drift from the code unless something checks them. A source-of-truth file is only useful if it stays true.
  • Test AI output on cases where you don't already know the answer. Getting the obvious case right proves little.
  • Design choices can make AI safer without making it more accurate: who sees which output, what the app says it doesn't judge, and whether a human reviews before release.

Why this matters beyond dance. Dance Coach gives people feedback on how their body moves and asks whether that analysis can be trusted. Physical therapy, fitness, and remote care products face the same questions. How accurate does movement feedback need to be before a patient sees it unreviewed? When must a clinician review it first? And how do you guide someone to record usable video at home?
